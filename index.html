Setup

Before You Begin: Get a Notebook

You are going to need a notebook. Any notebook. A composition book, a spiral notebook, something you already have sitting around, or something you buy specifically for this. It does not matter what it looks like.

You are going to use it for four things, one for each track.

Stabilize: Your non-negotiables list. The things your body and your life need from you on a hard day. You write this once and refer back to it.

Enjoy: Your enjoy menu. The things that restore you, bring you pleasure, or give you relief. You write this once and add to it whenever something new occurs to you.

Regulate: Your processing space. This is where you write when you are working through something emotionally. Unfiltered, unedited, just getting it out.

Act: Your action list. Every goal you want to eventually move toward, broken down into small steps. This is also where you cross things off when you complete them.

The notebook does not have to be organized or pretty. It just needs to exist and be somewhere you can find it.

What Is Happening

Why Everything Feels Impossible Right Now

Some situations at work get inside your system and stay there.

If you are working in a toxic environment, going through an HR investigation, navigating a performance plan, being excluded or retaliated against, preparing to leave a job that is hurting you, or recovering from a job loss you did not see coming, your nervous system is likely in sustained threat response.

When that happens, the brain floods with cortisol and the parts responsible for planning, decision making, and strategic thinking go offline. This is biology.1 It explains why you cannot think straight, why a simple email feels like too much, and why you know exactly what you need to do and still cannot make yourself do it.

Researchers call one version of this functional freeze.2 You are still moving through the day, making coffee, opening the laptop, going through the motions, but anything requiring higher order thinking feels impossible to start. You find yourself in loops, researching things that feel urgent but are not, doing anything that gives your brain stimulation without requiring you to look directly at the situation. This is a nervous system doing what it is built to do under sustained threat.

What you are grieving may be bigger than it looks from the outside. Beyond the immediate situation, you may have lost daily structure, a sense of professional purpose, the social world that existed around your work, trust in your own judgment, and the future you had assumed was coming. Every one of those is a real loss, and the grief that comes with them does not move in a straight line.3

Research on grief and recovery shows that people oscillate between processing what happened and moving forward.4 Some days there is energy and momentum. Other days there is nothing. Both are part of the same process, and neither means something has gone wrong.

Understanding the Experience

This Is Closer to the Real Experience

What follows is one person's observation drawn from personal and professional experience. It has not been formally studied or published. It is offered because it names something that is genuinely hard to find named anywhere else.

Most grief models were built for the death of a person, and they do not map onto what happens when work becomes a source of sustained threat or loss. The experience tends to be nonlinear and repetitive, and it carries a layer of identity disruption that standard models were never designed to address.

Grief is not linear. You can loop, pause, skip ahead, or revisit any stage.

Research on grief and recovery by Stroebe and Schut identifies that people oscillate between two orientations during recovery.4 In loss orientation you are processing what happened, sitting with the exhaustion, and grieving the identity disruption. In restoration orientation you are moving forward, planning, taking action, and exploring what comes next. Healthy recovery requires both, and moving between them is the process, not a detour from it.

Based on Stroebe and Schut's Dual Process Model of grief and bereavement.4

Before You Start

How to Use the Rating Scale

Throughout this guide and on the daily check-in you will rate how you are feeling on a scale of one to ten, where one is barely functioning and ten is fully resourced and clear.

The number is information, and once you have it, there is one question worth asking: what would move me one point higher?

Just one point.

That question identifies the next smallest useful action and keeps the goal realistic from wherever you are starting. If you are at a three, you are working toward a four, and that is enough. Use the rating as many times throughout the day as is useful, morning, midday, evening, or whenever you feel yourself shifting, because the goal is to build awareness of what moves the number and what depletes it.

The Framework

Four Tracks. Any Order. One Thing in Each.

When your nervous system is overloaded, you do not need a massive life plan. You need a structure that helps you get through today and take one clean step forward.

SERA is four tracks, and you do one thing in each of them every day. The order is yours. On the hardest days, when you have no idea where to begin, start with Stabilize, because the basics are always available even when nothing else is.

S Stabilize

Support your body first.

The basics that keep you functional on hard days. Before strategy is possible, the body needs basic support.

E Enjoy

Give your brain a moment of relief.

Pleasure, connection, and rest are how the brain produces what it needs to keep functioning. This is biology.

R Regulate

Settle your nervous system.

Coping skills and emotional processing tools drawn from evidence-based frameworks. One skill per day.

A Act

Take one clean step forward.

One action. Any size. The completion matters more than the scale.

S — Stabilize

The basics. The floor. Before anything else can hold, this has to.

Why This Track Exists

Stabilizing activities send direct safety signals to an overwhelmed or frozen nervous system. When the body completes a basic self-care task, gets outside, or eats something real, the nervous system receives evidence that the immediate threat has passed. These are physiological signals, and they are how you begin to move a dysregulated system back toward function.2

Whether you are navigating a toxic manager, waiting out an HR investigation, bracing for layoffs, surviving a PIP, or trying to function after a sudden job loss, your nervous system is managing more than it was designed to handle on a sustained basis.

There is also documented research on what happens cognitively when you do things like get dressed in real clothes, make your bed, or brush your teeth.5 The clothes you wear affect your psychological state and cognitive performance. Small completions prime the brain for further action. Doing the same basic tasks in the same order signals to the nervous system that today has structure, and a nervous system that perceives structure is less likely to stay in threat response.

These are unglamorous tasks, and that is the point. Some days getting dressed and eating something is enough. Both are valid starting points.

Once you know what your non-negotiables are, you stop deciding whether to do them. You just move through them.

Here are some examples to get you started, and your first activity is to write your own list in your notebook:

  • Taking a shower
  • Getting dressed in real clothes
  • Making your bed
  • Eating something real
  • Drinking water
  • Taking your medication
  • Feeding your pets
  • Stepping outside, even just to the mailbox
  • Doing one household task
  • Paying a bill that is due
  • Preparing what you need for the next workday

Some days you will move through the whole list. Other days you will do three things. The list removes the decision so you can just move through it.

E — Enjoy

Your brain is depleted. This track refuels it.

Why This Track Exists

Your brain produces four neurochemicals that drive motivation, connection, calm, and resilience. Dopamine drives anticipation, reward, and learning. Oxytocin drives connection, trust, and belonging. Serotonin drives calm, stability, and a sense of pride. Endorphins drive euphoria, pain relief, and emotional release. Sustained workplace stress and job loss deplete all four simultaneously. The brain produces these chemicals through experience, and deliberately choosing activities that generate them every day is one of the most direct biological interventions available to you.6

Many people in this situation feel guilty doing anything that feels good. They feel like they should be applying for jobs, fixing the situation, or at minimum suffering appropriately.

You are allowed to feel good right now.

The activities on this list are things that restore you, not things that numb you. There is a difference between watching a show you love and doom scrolling for four hours, between calling a friend who steadies you and venting in a loop that leaves you feeling worse. The question worth asking is whether you feel better after than you did before.

Here are some examples to get you started, and your first activity is to write your own enjoy menu in your notebook:

  • Watching something you love
  • Sitting with your pet
  • Calling someone who makes you feel like yourself
  • Going somewhere that feels good to be
  • Listening to music that moves you
  • Taking a walk with no destination or agenda
  • Reading something that has nothing to do with your situation
  • Laughing at something genuinely funny
  • Going to a coffee shop and just sitting

Pick one. Do it without guilt.

R — Regulate

This is where you build the skills to manage what is happening inside you.

Why This Track Exists

When the brain is in threat response, the prefrontal cortex, which governs rational thinking, planning, and decision making, goes partially offline. The amygdala, which processes threat, takes over. This is why emotional hijack makes it so hard to think clearly or make good decisions. Regulation skills work by activating the parasympathetic nervous system, which signals safety to the brain and allows the prefrontal cortex to come back online. Some skills do this physiologically through breathing and movement. Some do it cognitively through naming and reframing. Some do it through processing what happened so the emotional charge no longer floods the system. All of them are working on the same underlying neuroscience.7

By the time you get to this track, your body has some basic support and your brain has had a moment of relief. Regulation is where you tend to what is happening emotionally and cognitively, and it is what makes the Act track possible.

The skills in this track come from evidence-based frameworks including Dialectical Behavior Therapy, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, and Solution Focused Brief Therapy.7,8,9,10 These were developed specifically to help people manage emotional overwhelm, interrupt inaccurate thinking, process difficult experiences, and build psychological flexibility. You do not need to know anything about these frameworks to use the skills. You just need to pick one and do it.

Regulation looks different depending on what you need on a given day. Some days it means processing something painful. Some days it means slowing a thought spiral down. Some days it is five minutes of breathing before a hard meeting.

Your regulation activities might include things such as:

  • Writing without editing for fifteen minutes
  • Inhaling for four counts and exhaling for six counts2
  • Naming the emotion you are feeling and rating its intensity from one to ten
  • Grounding through your five senses
  • Identifying and naming a cognitive distortion that is running8
  • Practicing radical acceptance of something you cannot change right now7
  • Talking to someone safe
  • Writing your worst thought down and throwing it away11

The full Emotion Coping Skills Library contains 88 techniques organized by what you are feeling and what you need. Your first activity is to read through it and identify three skills you want to try this week. Having them chosen in advance means you are ready when you need them.

Pick one skill and give it ten to fifteen minutes.

A — Act

Here when you are ready for it.

Why This Track Exists

Every time you complete an action, however small, the brain releases dopamine. That dopamine signals reward and reinforces the behavior. Over time, repeated completions build what researchers call mastery, a documented sense of agency and capability that is one of the strongest predictors of recovery from destabilizing experiences.12 The brain responds similarly to completing a small task and a large one. This is why the size of the action matters far less than the completion. You are rebuilding the neurochemical foundation of forward movement one finished thing at a time.

One of the heaviest parts of being in this state is the weight of everything you know needs to happen and cannot make yourself do yet.

You are aware of all of it.

And your system does not have the capacity to move toward it right now, because when the nervous system is in sustained threat response, overwhelm, or freeze, the energy required for forward movement is simply unavailable. That has nothing to do with your character or your capability.

There is also a secondary layer that makes this harder. The awareness that you should be doing something, combined with the inability to do it, generates its own stress. That stress adds to the overwhelm. The overwhelm deepens the freeze. And the deeper the freeze, the louder the voice that says you should be doing something. It is a cycle that feeds itself, and it has nothing to do with how capable or motivated you actually are.

Research on grief, behavior change, and major life transitions consistently shows that forcing forward movement before the system is ready interrupts the process rather than accelerating it.4,13,14 The in-between space, where nothing feels solid and action feels impossible, is a necessary part of moving through something hard.

Understanding where you are right now matters here. Some days the nervous system is simply too flooded for forward movement, and pushing through it produces more stress. Other days there is a window, a moment of clarity or energy that feels different from the fog. When that window opens, the Act track is where it goes.

Act is yours to define, and it belongs to whatever phase you are in.

What this track offers is scaffolding. Instead of facing the full weight of everything that needs to happen, you name one goal, break it into the smallest possible pieces, and do one piece when you are ready. That structure allows you to feel like you are moving forward in some small way while you are still moving through the regulation and restoration process. The goal does not disappear.

It just becomes manageable.

Your action might include things such as:

  • Saving one screenshot or documenting what happened at work
  • Writing one note about what occurred and when
  • Opening your resume and reading it
  • Writing one resume bullet
  • Saving one job posting
  • Sending one email
  • Scheduling one appointment
  • Organizing one document or folder
  • Clearing one surface in your home
  • Taking any single step toward something you have been carrying
The Method

How to Scaffold a Goal

Start with the goal as one sentence. Write it in your notebook.

Step One
Name the goal.

Any goal. Write it down in one sentence. Something you want to eventually accomplish in any area of your life.

Step Two
Break it into pieces.

Ask yourself: what are all the things that need to happen for this goal to be complete? Write them all down without filtering.

Then take each of those pieces and break them down again into the smallest possible physical action. Keep breaking things down until each step is something you could do in five to ten minutes on its own.

Step Three
Do one piece.

Just one. Today, tomorrow, or next week. Whenever you are ready. Cross it off when you finish. That is your Act for that day.

Your Act for today is always the first step on the list. Nothing else. Just the first step.

Example One

Clean Out My Closet

The goal: clean out my closet.

That goal, sitting in your head, feels like one enormous thing. It has been sitting there for weeks or months, and every time you think about it you feel worse for not having done it. That is the weight of an unscaffolded goal.

First breakdown

  • Go through everything in the closet
  • Decide what to keep, donate, or throw away
  • Get rid of the donate and throw away items
  • Organize what is left

Second breakdown — go through everything in the closet becomes:

  • Open the closet door
  • Choose one section, shelf, or pile to start with
  • Pull that one thing out
  • Hold each item and decide: keep, donate, or throw away

Second breakdown — get rid of the donate items becomes:

  • Find a bag or box
  • Put the donate items in it
  • Look up a donation location near you
  • Put the bag by the door
  • Drive it there or schedule a pickup
Your Act for today: open the closet door and look at what is in there. That is it.
Example Two

Get a New Job

The goal: get a new job.

That goal, sitting in your head while your nervous system is flooded, is paralyzing. It is not one thing. It is fifty things tangled together. Here is what it looks like when you untangle it.

First breakdown

  • Update your resume
  • Update your LinkedIn
  • Identify what you are looking for
  • Reach out to your network
  • Find and apply to roles

Second breakdown — update your resume becomes:

  • Open the document and read it all the way through
  • Write down three things you want to update or add
  • Write one new bullet point
  • Update your contact information if it has changed
  • Save the updated version

Second breakdown — reach out to your network becomes:

  • Write down three people in your network you trust
  • Write one sentence about what you are looking for
  • Send one message to one person, not asking for a job, just reconnecting
  • Wait for a response before sending the next one
Your Act for today: open your resume and read it. Just read it. That is your Act for today, and it is enough.
